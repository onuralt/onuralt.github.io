Embedded Robotics

Tennis Ball Detecting and Tracking Robot

A more playful build in my portfolio: a tracked robot that uses a smartphone camera stream, PC-side OpenCV processing, Bluetooth commands, Arduino control, and regulated battery power to detect and track a tennis ball.

System Flow

Camera Stream to Motor Control

The robot splits perception and motion across the phone, PC, and Arduino. The phone streams frames over LAN, the PC detects the ball by fitting a circle to the filtered mask, then uses the circle center and radius to decide steering and distance before sending motor commands to the Arduino.

01 Capture Phone acts as an IP camera and streams live frames over LAN.
02 Filter PC converts RGB to HSV and thresholds the green ball.
03 Measure OpenCV fits a circle to the mask, using center offset and radius.
04 Decide Offset sign sets left/right; circle radius estimates distance.
05 Transmit PC sends the movement command through the HC-05 Bluetooth link.
06 Control Arduino MEGA converts serial commands into motor-driver signals.
07 Drive The motor driver moves the tracked chassis toward the ball.
08 Power Three LiPo cells feed regulated rails for logic and drive electronics.

OpenCV Filtering

RGB Frame to HSV Mask

The vision code captures the camera frame, resizes it, converts the RGB image to HSV, and applies tuned lower and upper HSV thresholds. This separates the green tennis ball from the background so OpenCV can find the largest contour and calculate the ball center.

Distance Estimate

Circle Radius as Range

After thresholding, OpenCV fits a circle around the detected white ball region. As the robot gets closer, the fitted circle grows in the frame, so its radius becomes an approximate distance signal.

Steering Error

Center Offset Drives Turning

The horizontal distance between the circle center and the frame center tells the controller which direction to turn. The motors keep correcting left or right until that offset falls under a chosen threshold.

Working Robot

Vision Loop Becomes Physical Motion

In this run, the PC keeps reading the phone camera stream, extracts the ball position, and sends correction commands over Bluetooth. The robot turns and advances as the circle center and radius change from frame to frame.

Tracking Behavior

Continuous Corrections Keep the Ball Centered

The working behavior is not a single command. Each frame updates the steering error, the PC sends a new serial instruction, and the motors keep correcting until the ball is close enough to the center of the frame and its radius indicates the desired range.

Digital Circuit Design

From Silicon Layers to Working Adders

I used Cadence to design and simulate two digital adders: the hardware blocks that let a processor add binary numbers. I started with small logic gates, drew both their circuit diagrams and chip-layout views, then combined them into two 16-bit adder designs.

Workflow: schematic design, physical layout, extracted views, waveform simulation, delay markers, and calculator measurements for leakage, static power, total power, and dynamic capacitance.

Layout Basics

Colors Become Chip Layers

A VLSI layout is drawn as stacked mask layers. Diffusion regions form transistor terminals, polysilicon crosses them to create gates, metal layers route signals, and contacts or vias connect one layer to the next.

This clean layer map separates the drawing conventions: contacts connect device layers to Metal1, while square vias connect one metal layer to the next metal layer.

metal1 metal2 metal3 polysilicon N diffusion P diffusion contacts vias

Multiplexer Enables the Skip Path

A multiplexer is a selector. In the carry-skip adder, it chooses whether a carry moves through the normal path or skips across a block, which is the idea that can make the adder faster than a simple ripple chain.

Design Perspective

Logic, Layout, and Metrics in One Flow

The project connected the full digital design flow: gate behavior, physical layout, extracted simulation, waveform verification, delay measurement, and power analysis. The measured result was not a one-line winner: CSKA showed lower delay and lower leakage/static readings, while MCCA showed lower total power in the tested waveform.

01 Cadence connected schematic design, layout, extraction, and simulation.
02 Small gates became reusable cells, then complete 16-bit adders.
03 CSKA and MCCA use different carry strategies, and the measured tradeoffs were mixed rather than one design winning every metric.
04 Waveforms and calculator measurements turned the layouts into measurable engineering results.
