Embedded Robotics

Tennis Ball Detecting and Tracking Robot

A more playful build in my portfolio: a tracked robot that uses a smartphone camera stream, PC-side OpenCV processing, Bluetooth commands, Arduino control, and regulated battery power to detect and track a tennis ball.

System Flow

Camera Stream to Motor Control

The robot splits perception and motion across the phone, PC, and Arduino. The phone streams frames over LAN, the PC detects the ball by fitting a circle to the filtered mask, then uses the circle center and radius to decide steering and distance before sending motor commands to the Arduino.

01 Capture Phone acts as an IP camera and streams live frames over LAN.
02 Filter PC converts RGB to HSV and thresholds the green ball.
03 Measure OpenCV fits a circle to the mask, using center offset and radius.
04 Decide Offset sign sets left/right; circle radius estimates distance.
05 Transmit PC sends the movement command through the HC-05 Bluetooth link.
06 Control Arduino MEGA converts serial commands into motor-driver signals.
07 Drive The motor driver moves the tracked chassis toward the ball.
08 Power Three LiPo cells feed regulated rails for logic and drive electronics.

OpenCV Filtering

RGB Frame to HSV Mask

The vision code captures the camera frame, resizes it, converts the RGB image to HSV, and applies tuned lower and upper HSV thresholds. This separates the green tennis ball from the background so OpenCV can find the largest contour and calculate the ball center.

RGB frame

HSV conversion

Threshold mask

Distance Estimate

Circle Radius as Range

After thresholding, OpenCV fits a circle around the detected white ball region. As the robot gets closer, the fitted circle grows in the frame, so its radius becomes an approximate distance signal.

Steering Error

Center Offset Drives Turning

The horizontal distance between the circle center and the frame center tells the controller which direction to turn. The motors keep correcting left or right until that offset falls under a chosen threshold.

Working Robot

Vision Loop Becomes Physical Motion

In this run, the PC keeps reading the phone camera stream, extracts the ball position, and sends correction commands over Bluetooth. The robot turns and advances as the circle center and radius change from frame to frame.

Tracking Behavior

Continuous Corrections Keep the Ball Centered

The working behavior is not a single command. Each frame updates the steering error, the PC sends a new serial instruction, and the motors keep correcting until the ball is close enough to the center of the frame and its radius indicates the desired range.

Digital Circuit Design

From Silicon Layers to Working Adders

This project was my hands-on introduction to VLSI design: drawing layout, connecting schematic behavior to physical layers, building adders from gates, and then using Cadence simulations to measure correctness, delay, noise, and power.

Layers to Logic

OR Gate Becomes Physical

The project starts at the cell level: first describe the gate as a schematic, then draw the physical layers that would implement it on silicon. The OR gate is the simplest example of that translation.

01 OR gate

The schematic defines the OR behavior with connected logic symbols, so the intended function is clear before layout work begins.

The layout turns that same OR behavior into routed diffusion, poly, metal, contacts, and vias for physical-rule and schematic-match checks.

Layers to Logic

XOR Carries More Structure

The XOR cell is more complex than the OR gate and appears repeatedly in the adders. It helps compute sum bits and propagate signals, so it has to be both logically correct and physically routable.

02 XOR gate

The XOR schematic provides the sum and propagate behavior used repeatedly inside the adder hierarchy.

The physical XOR layout shows why a more complex gate needs more routing and denser device placement than the OR cell.

Layers to Logic

MUX Enables the Skip Path

The MUX is the important control primitive for the carry-skip adder. It selects whether the carry ripples normally or skips across a block, then that selection behavior is implemented as layout.

03 MUX

The MUX schematic selects whether the carry should ripple normally or skip across a block.

The layout makes the selection circuit reusable as a compact physical building block for the skip path.

01 Full adder schematic

The one-bit schematic combines primitive gates into a complete sum-and-carry cell, the repeated unit of the carry-skip adder.

02 Full adder layout

The matching layout shows the same full-adder cell as routed physical geometry that can be reused in larger blocks.

Carry Skip Adder

One Bit Becomes Reusable

The carry-skip adder starts with one full-adder cell: a circuit that adds two input bits and an incoming carry. The schematic explains the logic; the layout shows that the same cell can be built physically.

03 Two-bit skip block

Two one-bit cells are grouped with skip logic, creating a block that can bypass normal ripple-carry movement when propagation conditions allow it.

04 Sixteen-bit schematic

The 16-bit schematic repeats the skip block across the full word, making the adder hierarchy and carry path visible at system scale.

Carry Skip Adder

The Hierarchy Expands

Once the cell is stable, the design scales through two-bit skip blocks into the full 16-bit adder. The skip block shortens carry movement when the logic allows it, which is the main idea behind CSKA.

05 Physical layout

The layout view shows the repeated CSKA blocks and long routed structure that later feed extracted verification and timing measurement.

Carry Skip Adder

Layout Closes the Loop

The final layout shows the repeated CSKA blocks as one physical circuit. This closes the loop from small gates to a complete 16-bit arithmetic unit.

Manchester Carry Chain

Propagate and Generate Control

The MCCA starts with a PG block: XOR produces propagate and AND produces generate. Those signals control a Manchester carry slice, where the local carry decision is made before the circuit is repeated.

01 PG logic

The PG block derives propagate and generate signals from the adder inputs, setting up the control logic that decides how carry moves.

02 Carry slice

The slice schematic shows how propagate and generate drive one local carry decision before the design is repeated into a chain.

Manchester Carry Chain

Slice Becomes Layout

After the Manchester slice is defined as logic, it is drawn as a physical layout. This view shows the device placement and routing that implement one carry stage.

03 Slice layout

The physical slice layout shows the routing and device placement needed to implement the carry decision in silicon layers.

Manchester Carry Chain

Slices Form a Four-Bit Chain

After the slice is verified, repeated slices form a four-bit chain. This intermediate step makes the carry path easier to follow before the design expands to the full 16-bit layout.

04 Four-bit chain

The four-bit schematic connects repeated slices so the carry chain can be inspected before composing the final adder width.

Manchester Carry Chain

Four Chains Become Sixteen Bits

The final Manchester carry-chain layout combines four chains into a 16-bit physical circuit. The long horizontal routing shows how the repeated carry structure spans the complete word.

05 Sixteen-bit layout

The 16-bit layout shows the completed MCCA as a routed physical design, with repeated carry structures visible across the full word.

01 Waveform verification

The simulation checks that the adder outputs settle to the expected logic values after switching, even when transition noise is visible.

Simulation Evidence

Correct Logic Inside Noisy Waveforms

The waveform view is dense because many sum bits switch together. Some traces show small noise and spikes around transitions, but the important check is whether each node settles to a valid logic level and the output matches the expected adder behavior.

Measurement

Numbers from the Waveforms

I measured worst-case timing and power after simulation. The all-high input case keeps the adder chain in propagation mode, which stresses the carry path and gives a useful comparison between CSKA and MCCA.

CSKA delay 293.99 ps HL / 318.27 ps LH

Marker difference between the input transition and the 16-bit CSKA carry output crossing.

MCCA delay 0.991 us HL / 0.990 us LH

Measured between the control/input marker and the MCCA carry output marker in the worst-case run.

Average total power from the calculator window; leakage is 8.178 nA, static power is 14.72 nW, and dynamic capacitance is 5.099 fF.

MCCA power 22.67 uW total

Calculator results show 416.8 nA leakage, 750.2 nW static power, and 338.3 fF dynamic capacitance.

Design Perspective

Logic, Layout, and Metrics in One Flow

The project connected circuit hierarchy with implementation evidence: gate-level behavior, physical layout, extracted simulation, delay measurement, and power analysis. Comparing CSKA and MCCA made the tradeoff visible across area, routing, switching activity, leakage, propagation delay, and waveform integrity.
